## Should an NDIS provider build, buy or extend its software?

**Buy first. For most providers the right answer is an established NDIS platform, extended with integrations and reporting where it falls short.** Mature NDIS platforms already handle participant records, service agreements, rostering, shift notes, incidents and claiming, and they absorb NDIA and Commission changes for every customer at once. A small provider building that from scratch would spend a great deal to recreate what a subscription already does.

Custom work earns its place around the platform. The table shows where each approach usually fits.

| Situation | Usually the best approach | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader or small team, common support types | Buy a platform, configure it well | Cheapest and fastest; the vendor carries the regulatory updates |
| Growing provider with payroll, accounting and rostering in separate tools | Buy, then integrate | The pain is retyping between systems, not the platform itself |
| Provider with reporting needs the platform can't meet | Buy, then add a reporting layer | A data warehouse and dashboards read from the platform's API or exports |
| Large or multi service provider whose workflows don't fit any platform | Build the core, or a platform plus substantial custom modules | Only when the fit gap costs more than building, and you can fund ongoing maintenance |
| Technology company building a product for providers | Build | You become the vendor, with the NDIA and Commission obligations that follow |

If you are that last case, note that the NDIS Commission states that from 1 July 2026 SIL providers and NDIS digital platform providers need to register with it. Whether a particular product is a digital platform for that purpose is a question for your lawyer, and it should be settled before you design the business model.

## What do the NDIS Practice Standards expect your systems to evidence?

**The Practice Standards set the quality outcomes registered providers must meet, and auditors check them against quality indicators, so your software has to produce the evidence on demand.** The Commission describes a core module for registered providers delivering higher risk supports, supplementary modules depending on the supports delivered, and a verification module for lower risk supports. Each module has participant focused outcomes and quality indicators that auditors use to assess compliance.

Software doesn't make a provider meet the standards, but poor software makes it hard to prove. A useful test is whether you could produce each of these in an audit within an hour:

- Every current service agreement, with the participant's signature or recorded agreement and the date.
- Progress notes for a chosen participant over a chosen period, showing who wrote and approved each one.
- The incident register with each incident's assessment, actions, notifications and closure.
- Complaints received, how each was handled and what changed as a result.
- Each worker's screening status, training records and the roles they were rostered into.
- Behaviour support plans and any restrictive practices used, matched against authorisations.
- Risk assessments and the reviews that followed them.

If any of those means opening five systems and a shared drive, that is where integration and reporting work pays for itself.

## How does incident management need to work?

**The NDIS Commission expects providers to identify, assess, record, manage and resolve incidents through an incident management system, and to notify reportable incidents within strict timeframes.** The Commission defines an incident broadly, as an act, omission, event or circumstance that has or could have caused harm to a person with disability, or where a person with disability has caused serious harm or the risk of it to another person.

| Reportable incident (registered providers) | Notify the Commission within |
|---|---|
| Death of a person with disability | 24 hours |
| Serious injury of a person with disability | 24 hours |
| Abuse or neglect of a person with disability | 24 hours |
| Unlawful sexual or physical contact with, or assault of, a person with disability | 24 hours |
| Sexual misconduct against, or in the presence of, a person with disability | 24 hours |
| Unauthorised restrictive practice, or one that doesn't follow a behaviour support plan | 5 business days (24 hours if it caused harm) |

Timeframes run from when the registered provider became aware. That shapes the software design:

1. **Capture on any device, immediately.** A worker on shift needs to record what happened from a phone in a few minutes, with the time of awareness stamped automatically.
2. **Classify with a person, not a model.** The system can prompt with the Commission's categories, but a trained person decides whether it is reportable.
3. **Show the clock.** Once flagged as reportable, the deadline is visible to the people responsible, with escalation if it is approaching.
4. **Keep the evidence together.** Notifications, follow up reports, actions and closure stay linked to the original record.
5. **Report on patterns.** Incident trends by service, site or type feed continuous improvement, which auditors also look for.

## What are the realities of claiming?

**Claiming runs through the NDIA's provider portals or its APIs, and the rules on timing and format matter as much as the software.** The NDIA says payment requests must be submitted within 2 years after a support is delivered, and for NDIA managed participants it recommends claiming within 90 days, because later claims get extra checks and may be held for review for up to 28 days.

The practical points for system design, as the NDIA describes them at the time of writing (November 2026):

- **Two portals.** The my NDIS provider portal is used for participants on the NDIA's new computer system, and the myplace provider portal for participants on the old system. Staff access the portals with myID linked to the business through Relationship Authorisation Manager (RAM). Older guidance refers to PRODA, so check the NDIA's current page when setting up access.
- **Bulk upload is unavoidable for some participants.** Single claims can be made through the payment request tile in myplace, but the bulk payment request upload must be used for participants with plans in the new computer system. Your platform or integration needs to generate that file accurately, with participant numbers, support item numbers, dates and prices that match the plan.
- **API access is gated.** Registered providers and plan managers can apply to integrate directly with the NDIA's APIs through its digital partner process. Software developers must partner with a registered provider and complete additional cyber security activities.
- **Rejected claims need a loop.** Each rejection should come back into your system with its reason, so it is fixed and resubmitted rather than lost.

Payment terms reward getting this right: the NDIA says valid claims from providers recorded as "my providers" are usually paid within 2 to 3 business days.

## Why is SCHADS rostering hard to automate?

**Because the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Award has many rules that interact: shift types, minimum engagements, broken shifts, sleepovers, travel, overtime and penalty rates, and they apply differently across employee classifications.** Generic rostering and payroll tools often handle the common cases and get the edge cases wrong, which shows up as underpayment risk.

If rostering, timesheets and payroll sit in different systems, an integration that applies the award consistently, and flags shifts that need a person's review, is often more valuable than any new feature. The rules come from the award as published by the Fair Work Ombudsman, and your payroll adviser should sign off the interpretation before it is coded. ## How should participant information be protected?

**Treat every participant record as sensitive information, because much of it is health information, and because the Privacy Act's small business exemption doesn't apply to businesses that provide a health service and hold health information.** That has direct consequences for the software:

- Role based access so workers see only the participants they support.
- An audit trail of who viewed and changed each record.
- Australian hosting for records, files, backups and any AI processing.
- Retention and destruction rules that match your obligations.
- A tested process for notifiable data breaches.

## Where does AI help, and where are the red lines?

**AI is useful for drafting and reading, for staff to check, and it should stay out of decisions about people's safety and rights.**

| Good uses, with human review | Red lines |
|---|---|
| Drafting a progress note from a worker's dictated summary | Writing notes without the worker reviewing them |
| Reading referral documents and plans to prefill participant details | Deciding whether an incident is reportable |
| Summarising a participant's recent notes before a review meeting | Any decision about restrictive practices |
| Checking claims against service agreements before upload | Submitting claims without a person approving the batch |
| Answering staff questions from your own policies and procedures | Giving participants or families clinical or legal advice |
