## Who owns the attendee relationship: your ticketing platform or you?

**Whoever holds the attendee's contact details, purchase history and marketing consent owns the relationship, and that is often the platform.** It's the first question to settle, because it decides what you can build later.

Broadly, Australian organisers choose between three kinds of platform:

| Platform type | Examples | Good at | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self service ticketing | Humanitix, Eventbrite | Fast setup, low fixed cost, good checkout | How much attendee data you can export, and in what format |
| Venue and promoter ticketing | Ticketek, Ticketmaster, Moshtix, Oztix | Reserved seating, high demand on sales, resale exchanges | Contract terms on data, fees and exclusivity |
| Conference and registration | Cvent, Swapcard, Whova | Agendas, exhibitors, networking, badges | Cost per event and per attendee, and integration with your CRM |

If you run many events a year, the useful custom layer is usually a CRM that imports every event's attendees, keeps consent per person, and lets you market the next event without starting from a blank list. Under APP 7, covered businesses using personal information for direct marketing must give people a simple way to opt out, so consent has to come across with the data.

## What does your system have to enforce on ticket resale?

**In NSW, restricted tickets can't be resold for more than the original cost plus transaction costs capped at 10% of the ticket cost.** The NSW Government says the laws apply to tickets for sporting or entertainment events held in NSW that carry a resale restriction, meaning a ticket condition that limits or prohibits resale.

Any advertisement to resell such a ticket must include:

1. The original ticket cost.
2. An asking price no more than 10% above the original cost.
3. The location the holder is authorised to view from, such as bay, row or seat number.

Publication owners, including resale sites, social media and marketplaces, commit an offence by publishing a noncompliant ad. NSW suggests steps such as checking ads before listing, requiring proof of original purchase and writing official original prices into the website's code.

For an organiser or venue, that translates into features: a resale restriction flag on each ticket type, an official resale exchange that enforces the cap, named tickets where the risk justifies it, purchase limits per buyer, and records of original prices that resale partners can check. Other states set their own rules, so a national tour needs the conditions checked state by state.

## What happens to refunds when an event changes?

**If you cancel or make a major change, attendees are generally entitled to a refund.** The ACCC gives examples of a major change: replacing a festival's headline act, or moving a show from one city to another. Consumers may also claim compensation for related losses such as travel and accommodation, depending on the situation. Where a cancellation is caused only by someone else, such as government restrictions, the ACCC says remedies depend on the ticket terms.

The software requirement is a bulk refund and communication process: identify every affected order across channels, refund through the original payment method, send a clear message, and record what was offered. Doing that from a spreadsheet for 8,000 tickets is where organisers lose goodwill.

## How should ticket prices and booking fees be displayed?

**Show the total price upfront today, and from 1 July 2027 show any per transaction fee right beside the base price.** The ACCC's guidance for ticket sellers already says not to engage in drip pricing, and its price display rules require a single total including unavoidable fees. The Unfair Trading Practices Act 2026 adds a specific obligation from 1 July 2027: where a base price is displayed for consumers, any transaction based charge (a booking or service fee charged per order) must be displayed in close proximity, saying how much it is, that it is per transaction, and whether the base price includes it.

If you run your own checkout, the price component on every event page and seat map needs to show fees from the first screen. If a platform runs it, ask how it will comply before July 2027.

## How do accreditation and access control work for staff, crew and exhibitors?

**Accreditation is a permissions system: who is allowed where, when, and on whose authority.** Ticketing platforms handle the public; accreditation handles everyone else, often thousands of people at a large event. A useful system covers:

- Applications or bulk uploads by each contractor, with approval by the right manager
- Zones and times per credential (back of house, stage, media pit, catering)
- Photo capture and checks, plus induction completion and licence or clearance records where your safety plan needs them
- Printing or mobile credentials with scannable codes
- Instant revocation, so a lost or misused pass stops working at every gate
- Scan logs per gate for later review

If you scan IDs to issue credentials, the OAIC's ID scanner guidance applies: scan only when sighting isn't enough, explain it in your privacy policy, and delete scans on a schedule.

## What should an exhibitor and sponsor portal include?

**One login where each exhibitor or sponsor sees what they bought, what they owe you and what you owe them.** For conferences and trade shows, that means stand details and floor plans, deliverable deadlines (logos, artwork, staff names, power and furniture orders), invoices and payment status, and lead retrieval: scanning attendee badges and exporting leads after the event. Lead retrieval has a privacy angle; attendees should know when scanning their badge shares their details with an exhibitor. ## What about run sheets and incident logs on the day?

**Run sheets and incident logs work best as one shared live system rather than PDFs and radio calls.** A run sheet app shows each team its cues with live timing changes. An incident log records what happened, where, who responded, and any follow up, with photos and timestamps. Your event safety plan, venue conditions and WHS advisers decide what has to be logged; the software makes logging quick enough that staff actually do it, including offline when mobile networks are congested by the crowd.

## When do attendees actually download an event app?

**When the event runs over several days and the app does something a web page can't.** Conferences with personal agendas, networking and session changes are good candidates. One night events rarely are. For most organisers, a fast mobile web experience with tickets, maps and timings is cheaper and reaches everyone. Our [web app vs mobile app guide](/guides/web-app-vs-mobile-app) works through the decision.

## Where does AI fit in event operations?

**In attendee questions, session matchmaking and post event reporting.** An assistant that answers from the event's own information (times, venue access, accessibility, refund terms) handles the volume of repeat questions before and during an event, with staff taking anything unusual. For conferences, matching attendees to sessions or exhibitors based on stated interests is a contained model. After the event, AI can summarise survey comments and incident logs into themes for the debrief.

## What do event software builds cost?

**Most organisers need one or two modules alongside their ticketing platform.** These are indicative AUD market ranges, ex GST, not quotes, consistent with the bands Expeed publishes for Australian custom software.

| Build | Indicative range |
|---|---|
| Multi event CRM fed from ticketing exports | $20,000 to $50,000 |
| Accreditation and credential system with gate scanning | $30,000 to $90,000 |
| Exhibitor portal with lead capture | $40,000 to $100,000 |
| Run sheet and incident log app for event staff | $20,000 to $50,000 |

Costs rise with the number of credential types and gates, offline requirements and the ticketing platform's API limits.
