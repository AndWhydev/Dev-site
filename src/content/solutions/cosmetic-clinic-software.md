## What changed for cosmetic practice, and why your software has to know

**On 1 July 2023 the Medical Board of Australia issued guidelines for medical practitioners who perform cosmetic surgery and procedures, and they turned several clinic habits into rules a system can check.** The guidelines have separate sections for cosmetic surgery and for non surgical procedures such as injectables. Ahpra then added guidelines for advertising higher risk non surgical cosmetic procedures, in effect from 2 September 2025.

The result is that a cosmetic clinic now has three kinds of record a hairdresser's booking app was never designed for: which prescriber consulted which patient before each injectable treatment, what the patient consented to and when, and whether each photo may be used and for what. Beauty booking products handle the calendar well. Clinical products built for aesthetics, including Pabau, Clinicminds and Cliniko, go further. The gaps that remain are where custom work fits.

## Injectables are prescription medicines

**Each Schedule 4 injectable treatment must trace back to a prescriber consult with that patient.** The Board's guidelines say medical practitioners must consult the patient, in person or by video, each time they prescribe a Schedule 4 cosmetic injectable, and must comply with their state or territory's drugs and poisons laws on permits, prescribing, records, supply, storage and transport. Where another registered practitioner administers the injectable and is not an authorised prescriber, the prescribing doctor remains responsible for the patient, including post procedure care. The prescriber also needs formal alternative arrangements, with a nominated delegate, when they are not available afterwards.

The guidelines also require the prescriber to obtain informed consent, assess the patient's reasons and motivations, screen for underlying psychological conditions such as body dysmorphic disorder, and refer the patient for independent evaluation where needed. They state that botulinum toxin and dermal fillers should not be prescribed to patients under 18 for cosmetic purposes.

A system that supports this links four things for every treatment:

1. The prescriber consult: date, mode (in person or video), practitioner and outcome.
2. The prescription: product type and quantity.
3. The administration: who injected, batch, site and amount.
4. The aftercare contact: who is responsible if complications arise.

If any link is missing, the treatment shouldn't be bookable. That single check makes the prescriber requirement visible at the front desk, where gaps usually start.

## Why your booking site copy is a compliance risk

**Your website, booking pages and social accounts are advertising, and three rule sets apply to them at once.** The TGA's guidance says prescription medicines are prohibited from being advertised to the public, and that most cosmetic injectables contain prescription only substances. It warns that content implying the effect of a prescription medicine, such as before and after images or treatment progress, is likely to be prohibited, and that promoting a telehealth service as a way to obtain particular prescription medicines is likely to be prohibited too.

Ahpra's rules sit on top. Testimonials are not permitted in any advertising of a regulated health service, and a clinic's own social media pages count as advertising. Ahpra's resources for the higher risk non surgical guidelines say a patient or influencer cannot post about their procedure and tag the clinic, because the tag makes the post part of the clinic's advertising. Where before and after images are allowed for these procedures, they must not show anyone under 18, must be actual patients of the advertising practitioner, must be realistic, and must not make the after image the most prominent.

What software can do here is modest but useful:

- Keep booking page treatment names in a managed list your adviser has approved, not free text.
- Block reviews and testimonial widgets on clinical pages.
- Stop photos without an advertising consent from ever reaching the website or social scheduler.
- Keep a dated record of what each page said, in case a regulator asks.

## Consent, cooling off and referrals for surgery

**Cosmetic surgery workflows are where the Board's rules are most specific, and easiest to enforce in software.** All patients seeking cosmetic surgery must have a referral, preferably from their usual GP, from a practitioner who works independently of the surgeon. They must have at least two pre operative consultations. They cannot consent until they have had an in person consultation with the surgeon. After two consultations and signed consent, there must be a cooling off period of at least seven days before the surgery can be booked or a deposit paid, and no money should be payable until it ends, other than for initial consultations. For patients under 18, the cooling off period is at least three months. All cosmetic surgery must be performed in a facility accredited to Commission standards specified by the Board.

| Rule | What the system checks |
|---|---|
| Referral | Referral attached, referrer independent of the surgeon |
| Two consultations | Both recorded, first with the surgeon or a registered practitioner working with them |
| In person consult | Consent form unavailable until it is recorded |
| Seven day cooling off | Surgery booking and deposit blocked until day eight |
| Under 18 | Three month wait applied automatically from the date of birth |
| Facility | Booking only into an accredited facility |

For non surgical procedures, patients under 18 need a cooling off period of at least seven days between consent and the procedure. The [specialist practice software](/solutions/specialist-medical-practice-software) page covers referral handling in more depth.

## Before and after photos: storage, consent and social use

**Every clinical photo needs a consent attached to it, and the consent has to say what the photo may be used for.** The Board's guidelines say consent must be requested for any photographs or videos the practitioner proposes to take during a consultation or procedure. Separately, clinics that provide health services are covered by the Privacy Act regardless of turnover, so photos are part of the health record and need the same protection.

A photo vault with consent tagging stores each image against the patient, treatment and date, records consent per purpose (clinical record, de identified education, advertising), and refuses to export an image for a purpose the patient hasn't agreed to. Staff phones stop being a storage location. Access is logged and hosting stays in an Australian region. ## Booking platforms versus clinical records

**Keep the calendar and the clinical record joined, but don't let the booking tool become the record.** Many clinics start on a beauty booking app and add clinical forms. That works until a regulator or insurer asks for the prescriber consult behind a treatment. A clinical product designed for aesthetics is usually the better base, with custom work limited to the prescriber telehealth step, S4 stock reconciliation across sites and consent tagging.
