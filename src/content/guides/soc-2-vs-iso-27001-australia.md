## What is the difference between SOC 2 and ISO 27001?

**ISO/IEC 27001 is a certification that your information security management system (ISMS) meets an international standard; SOC 2 is an auditor's report on your security controls against criteria set by the American Institute of CPAs (AICPA).** One certifies how you manage security as a system. The other gives a detailed opinion on specific controls, which customers read.

| | ISO/IEC 27001 | SOC 2 |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | International standard for an ISMS, current edition ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (with one amendment) | Attestation examination under AICPA standards, against the Trust Services Criteria |
| Who issues it | A certification body, ideally accredited by an IAF recognised accreditation body such as JAS-ANZ in Australia and New Zealand | A licensed CPA firm |
| Output | A certificate, usually public, naming the scope | A detailed report, typically shared under NDA |
| What is tested | That the ISMS meets the standard's clauses, with risk based selection of Annex A controls | The system description and controls for the criteria in scope: security, plus optionally availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy |
| Variants | One certificate; scope varies | Type 1 (design, point in time) or Type 2 (design and operating effectiveness over a period) |
| Cycle | Certificate with annual surveillance audits and periodic recertification | Commonly a new Type 2 report each year |
| Strongest in | Australia, Europe, Asia, government adjacent buyers | United States, and buyers with US style vendor risk programs |

Two points of detail matter in 2026. ISO's own guidance says to refer to certification with the full reference, "certified to ISO/IEC 27001:2022", and the transition from the older edition has finished, so a certificate to the 2013 or 2017 version is out of date. And the AICPA published exposure drafts of proposed 2027 Trust Services Criteria on 26 October 2026, so SOC 2 criteria are likely to change for reports covering periods from 2027 onward.

## Which do Australian buyers ask for?

**Australian buyers commonly recognise both. ISO 27001, as an international standard certified by locally accredited bodies, tends to be the more familiar name to Australian procurement teams, while SOC 2 Type 2 is the usual request wherever the buyer runs a US style vendor risk program.** Neither is required by Australian law. They show up in procurement as a way to shortcut a long security questionnaire. Use this matrix to decide which to pursue first.

| Your situation | Get first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Selling mostly to Australian and New Zealand mid market and enterprise | ISO/IEC 27001 | Widely recognised, public certificate, local accredited certification bodies |
| US customers or US headquartered buyers are a big part of the pipeline | SOC 2 Type 2 | It is the report US procurement teams most commonly request |
| Selling into European or Asian enterprises | ISO/IEC 27001 | International standard with broad recognition |
| APRA regulated buyers (banks, insurers, super funds) | Either, plus evidence for CPS 234 | APRA's standard puts the onus on the regulated entity to assess your controls; a report or certificate helps but won't answer everything |
| Federal government | IRAP and Essential Eight first | Agencies work from ASD's ISM and their own policies; see below |
| Early stage, no enterprise deals yet | Neither yet | Build the controls and evidence now; certify when a deal needs it |

If an enterprise prospect sends a security questionnaire, ask early whether ISO 27001 or a SOC 2 Type 2 would let you skip it. The answer tells you which to buy.

For APRA regulated buyers, our guide to [APRA CPS 234 and AI](/guides/apra-cps-234-ai) explains what they must assess in a service provider. Fintechs receiving bank data under the [Consumer Data Right](/guides/consumer-data-right-software) have separate security controls and assurance reporting set by the CDR Rules.

### A worked timeline for a 30 person SaaS company

This is an illustrative plan, not a quote. It assumes a product already running on a major cloud platform with reasonable practices, and a goal of both frameworks within 18 months.

| Month | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Gap assessment against ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and SOC 2 security criteria; agree scope (product, cloud accounts, people) |
| 2 to 5 | Write the ISMS: risk assessment, Statement of Applicability, policies; fix control gaps in the platform and the pipeline |
| 5 to 6 | Internal audit and management review |
| 6 to 8 | ISO 27001 certification audit (stage 1 and stage 2) |
| 6 to 12 | SOC 2 Type 2 observation period, reusing the same controls and evidence |
| 13 to 14 | SOC 2 Type 2 report issued |
| 12 onward | ISO surveillance audits and annual SOC 2 reports |

The overlap is real. Most controls an ISO 27001 auditor checks are the same ones a SOC 2 auditor tests, so doing one first makes the second much cheaper.

## What actually happens in each audit?

**ISO 27001 certification is a two stage audit of your management system; a SOC 2 examination is a test of your controls against criteria, with a written opinion at the end.** Knowing the steps helps you plan staff time, which is where most of the cost sits.

**ISO/IEC 27001 certification**

1. **Define the scope.** Which products, locations, teams and cloud accounts are inside the ISMS. A narrow scope is cheaper but must still make sense to the customers who read the certificate.
2. **Build the ISMS.** A risk assessment method, a risk register, a risk treatment plan, a Statement of Applicability saying which Annex A controls apply and why, and the policies and procedures that support them.
3. **Operate it.** Run the controls for long enough to produce records: access reviews, supplier reviews, incident logs, training records.
4. **Internal audit and management review.** The standard requires both before certification.
5. **Stage 1 audit.** The certification body reviews documentation and readiness.
6. **Stage 2 audit.** The certification body tests that the ISMS is implemented and effective, samples evidence and raises any nonconformities, which must be closed before the certificate issues.
7. **Surveillance and recertification.** Annual surveillance audits keep the certificate current, with a full recertification at the end of the cycle.

**SOC 2 examination**

1. **Choose the criteria.** Security is always in scope. Add availability, processing integrity, confidentiality or privacy only if customers need them, because each adds testing.
2. **Write the system description.** A narrative of the services, infrastructure, software, people, procedures and data, against the AICPA description criteria.
3. **Readiness assessment.** Optional, but most first time companies do one to find gaps before the clock starts.
4. **Type 1 or observation period.** A Type 1 tests design at a date. A Type 2 needs the controls to operate through an observation period, then the auditor tests samples from across it.
5. **Report.** The CPA firm issues its opinion, the system description, the controls and test results, and any exceptions. Exceptions don't automatically mean a failed report, but buyers read them.

### How do you choose an auditor or certification body?

**Pick on credibility first and price second, because the buyer is the one who has to trust the result.** Questions worth asking:

- For ISO 27001, is the certification body accredited for ISO/IEC 27001 by JAS-ANZ or another IAF member accreditation body? An unaccredited certificate may not satisfy an enterprise buyer.
- For SOC 2, is the firm a licensed CPA firm, and are the practitioners who sign the report licensed and peer reviewed? The AICPA has publicly cautioned about quick turnaround SOC engagements.
- Have they audited SaaS businesses on your cloud platform, and do they accept evidence from your compliance platform if you use one?
- What is included: readiness work, stage 1 and stage 2, surveillance years, report drafting, retesting of exceptions?
- Can the same evidence set serve both frameworks if you plan to hold both?

## How does the way you build the product affect the audit?

**Auditors don't test intentions; they sample evidence, and engineering practice decides whether that evidence exists or has to be reconstructed by hand.** A product built with these habits is much faster to certify:

1. **Infrastructure as code.** Every cloud resource defined in version controlled templates gives you change history, peer review and a reproducible environment, which covers several change management and configuration controls at once.
2. **Every change through a pull request.** Required review, linked tickets and automated tests on every merge are the evidence for change control and secure development.
3. **Logged, automated deployments.** A pipeline that records who deployed what and when, with no manual production changes, answers the most common sampling request.
4. **Single sign on and least privilege.** Central identity, multi factor authentication and role based access make quarterly access reviews a report rather than a spreadsheet project.
5. **Centralised logging and alerting.** Retained, tamper resistant logs for production access and security events.
6. **Backups that are restored.** Scheduled restore tests, recorded, because "we have backups" isn't evidence.
7. **Vulnerability management in the pipeline.** Dependency and container scanning with tracked remediation times.
8. **Separate environments, no production data in test.** This is a frequent finding and an easy one to avoid at build time.
9. **A sub-processor register.** Every SaaS tool and AI provider that touches customer data, with its own assurance report or certificate on file.

## Where do Essential Eight and IRAP fit?

**Essential Eight and IRAP are Australian Government frameworks from the Australian Signals Directorate, and they answer different questions from SOC 2 and ISO 27001.** They are complementary, not alternatives.

| Framework | Owner | What it is | When you'll meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essential Eight | ASD | Eight mitigation strategies with a maturity model | Required of many federal entities under government policy; sometimes asked of their suppliers |
| IRAP | ASD | Independent assessment by an ASD endorsed assessor, typically against the Information Security Manual | Selling cloud or software that handles government information |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | ISO and IEC | Certified management system | Enterprise and international procurement |
| SOC 2 | AICPA | CPA attestation on controls | US and US style procurement |

An ISO 27001 ISMS is a good home for Essential Eight controls: your risk assessment can adopt the strategies and maturity targets, and your auditors will see them as part of the system. IRAP is a separate assessment with its own scope and assessors. ## When is neither worth it yet?

**If no buyer has asked and you have no enterprise deals in the pipeline, put the money into controls, not certificates.** Certification makes sense when it unblocks revenue. Before that:

- Build the engineering practices above.
- Write a short security overview document and keep a standard questionnaire response ready.
- Run an independent penetration test before your first enterprise sale.
- Choose cloud providers and sub-processors that already hold ISO 27001 and SOC 2, so you can rely on their reports for the layers they run. If you're still moving onto the cloud, our [cloud migration cost guide](/guides/cloud-migration-cost-australia) covers landing zones built with this evidence in mind.

Then, when the first large deal asks, you are months from certification, not a year.
