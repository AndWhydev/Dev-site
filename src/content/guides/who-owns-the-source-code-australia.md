## Who owns source code by default under Australian law?

**The author of source code owns the copyright in it by default, unless they wrote it as an employee in the course of their employment.** The Copyright Act 1968 treats a computer program as a literary work, defining it as a set of statements or instructions to be used directly or indirectly in a computer to bring about a certain result. Section 35(2) makes the author of a literary work the owner of the copyright in it.

The main exception is in section 35(6): where a work is made by an author under the terms of their employment by another person under a contract of service or apprenticeship, the employer owns the copyright. Section 35(3) lets parties exclude or modify that rule by agreement, which is why some employment contracts deal with it expressly.

Copyright arises automatically when the code is written. There is no registration system for copyright in Australia, so ownership turns on who wrote the code, in what capacity, and what the paperwork says.

This page is general information, not legal advice. For a specific contract or dispute, talk to a lawyer who works in technology or IP.

## Employees, contractors and agencies: why does the answer differ?

**Employees' code belongs to the employer; contractors, freelancers and agencies keep theirs unless they assign it in writing.** The key legal question is whether the person writing the code works under a contract of service (an employee) or a contract for services (a contractor).

| Who writes the code | Default owner under the Copyright Act | What you need |
|---|---|---|
| Your employee, as part of their job | You, the employer (s 35(6)) | A clear employment contract; moral rights consents |
| Your employee, on their own time and outside their role | Usually the employee | A written assignment if you want it |
| A freelancer or contractor you engage | The freelancer | A written, signed assignment to you |
| An agency's employees | The agency, as their employer | A written assignment from the agency to you |
| An agency's subcontractors | The subcontractor, unless they've assigned to the agency | The agency must hold assignments from them before it can assign to you |
| A cofounder before any agreement | The cofounder personally | An assignment to the company, ideally at incorporation |

The agency row trips people up. An agency can only assign what it owns, so if it used freelancers who never assigned their work to the agency, there is a gap in the chain. Ask how the vendor makes sure everyone who touches your code has assigned their rights to it.

## Does paying for software mean you own it?

**No. Paying for code doesn't transfer copyright; a written, signed assignment does.** Section 196(3) of the Copyright Act says an assignment of copyright, whether total or partial, does not have effect unless it is in writing signed by or on behalf of the assignor.

If you paid without a written assignment, you are not left with nothing. Depending on the circumstances, you may have an implied licence to use the software for the purpose it was commissioned. But a licence to use is not ownership: you may not be able to stop the developer reusing or selling the same code, and you may struggle to modify it, sell it with your business or license it to others.

Section 197 helps here. It allows a signed agreement to assign copyright that doesn't exist yet, so a contract signed before work starts can make ownership pass automatically when the code is created or when a stated condition is met, such as payment.

## What should an IP assignment clause say?

**A good assignment clause says what is assigned, when it passes to you, what the developer keeps, and what licence you get over anything the developer keeps.**

Check for each of these:

1. **Scope.** Source code, object code, designs, documentation, database schemas, configuration and build scripts, not just "the software".
2. **Timing.** Common triggers are on creation or on payment for each deliverable. If ownership passes on payment, check each stage passes as it is paid, not only at the end.
3. **Background IP.** Developers keep their existing tools, libraries and know how. You need a perpetual, royalty free licence to use, modify and maintain any background IP built into your software, including through another developer later.
4. **No reclassification.** Code written for you shouldn't be treated as background IP just because it could be reused elsewhere.
5. **Chain of title.** The vendor promises that its staff and subcontractors have assigned their rights to it.
6. **Moral rights consents.** See the next section.
7. **Further assurance.** The vendor will sign any further document needed to evidence the assignment.
8. **Handover.** When you receive the repository, credentials and accounts, and what you must have paid first.

Assignment and licence are different things, and both have a place:

| | Assignment | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | Ownership of the copyright | Permission to use it on stated terms |
| Can you stop others using it? | Yes | Only if the licence is exclusive |
| Can you sell it with your business? | Yes | Only if the licence allows transfer |
| Typical use | Code written specifically for you | The developer's background tools and third party components |

## What are moral rights in software, and how are they handled?

**Moral rights are personal rights of the individual people who wrote the code, and they can't be assigned, so contracts handle them with written consents.** They are set out in Part IX of the Copyright Act and include the right of attribution and the right of integrity, which lets an author object to derogatory treatment of their work.

The Act says a moral right is not transmissible by assignment. So even a perfect IP assignment leaves the programmers with rights that could, in principle, be used to object to how their code is changed. The fix is consent. Section 195AWA says it isn't an infringement of a moral right to do something within the scope of a written consent genuinely given by the author. A consent must relate to specified acts or classes of acts and to specified works or works of a particular description, but an employee can consent for the benefit of their employer in relation to all works made in the course of their employment.

In practice, the vendor should obtain consents from everyone who writes your code, covering modification, adaptation, use without attribution and commercialisation, and promise in the contract that it has done so.

## What about existing code, libraries and open source?

**Almost every application includes code the developer didn't write for you, and you can't be assigned rights the developer doesn't own.** What matters is that you know what is in your software and what licence each part comes with.

- **The developer's background code.** Covered by the licence described above.
- **Commercial components.** Paid libraries, themes or services with their own licence terms and fees.
- **Open source.** Most open source licences are permissive and only ask for attribution. Copyleft licences go further: the Free Software Foundation describes copyleft as a method that requires all modified and extended versions of a program to be free as well. Depending on the licence and how the software is distributed, that can oblige you to release source code.

Ask for a list of the main third party and open source components and their licences at handover, and a promise that copyleft code won't be included in a way that forces disclosure of your code without your approval.

## Who owns code generated by AI?

**At the time of writing, the position in Australia is unsettled, but copyright requires a human author, so code produced by AI with little human input may not be protected.** This matters more every year as developers use AI coding tools.

The clearest authority is Telstra Corporation Limited v Phone Directories Company Pty Ltd [2010] FCAFC 149, where the Full Federal Court held that directories largely produced by automated processes were not original works because they lacked human authorship. The principle carries over: the more a person shapes the code, the stronger the case that copyright exists and can be assigned.

The government is working on it. The Attorney-General's Department says that in October 2025 the government asked its Copyright and AI Reference Group to explore three priorities, including improving certainty on how copyright law applies to material generated through the use of AI. It also noted that the government is not considering a text and data mining exception.

What to do now:

- **Contract for it.** Make the assignment cover AI assisted work to the extent rights exist in it.
- **Check the tools' terms.** Each AI coding tool has its own terms about outputs and about what it can do with your data. Our guide to [making an app built with Lovable, Bolt or Replit production ready](/guides/make-ai-built-app-production-ready) gives one example.
- **Rely on more than copyright.** Confidentiality clauses, access control and keeping the repository in your control protect code whether or not copyright subsists in every line.

## It isn't only the code: who should own the accounts?

**Your business should own every account the software depends on, even if the developer sets them up.** Losing access to one of these can stop a launch or an update as surely as losing the code.

| Asset | Who should own it | How hard it is to move later |
|---|---|---|
| Code repository | You, or transferred to you at a defined point | Easy if the contract says when |
| Domain name and DNS | You | Moderate; needs the registrar login |
| Cloud hosting and database | You | Moderate to hard; data and settings must move |
| Apple App Store listing | You | Apple says the sending Account Holder must start the transfer and the recipient accepts it; some data, such as past sales reports, stays behind |
| Google Play listing | You | Google says the target account owner must approve the transfer; earnings reports don't move |
| Payment provider | You | Hard; usually means a new account and new customer billing |
| Email, SMS and analytics services | You | Moderate |
| API keys and secrets | You, stored in a secrets manager | Easy to rotate if documented |
| Design files and documentation | You | Easy if they're in a shared workspace |

## What if a developer won't hand over the code?

**Start with the contract and a calm written request; escalate only if that fails.** The right next step depends on what was agreed and what has been paid.

1. **Read the contract.** Find the assignment clause, the handover clause and any condition, such as final payment.
2. **List what you need**: repository, database export, credentials, account transfers and documentation.
3. **Ask in writing**, referring to the clause, with a reasonable deadline.
4. **Pay what is genuinely owed**, or put the disputed amount aside, if ownership passes on payment.
5. **Secure what you can control now**: change passwords on accounts in your name and take backups.
6. **Get help.** The Australian Small Business and Family Enterprise Ombudsman responds to small businesses in dispute with other businesses, and provides information on dispute resolution options and access to mediation. A technology lawyer can advise on your position.

If there was never a written assignment, the developer may own the code, and the conversation becomes a negotiation to buy it or a decision to rebuild.
