## Do you need a technical cofounder? The short answer by stage

**Whether you need a technical cofounder depends on what stage you're at and whether software is the business or a tool the business uses.** The table below is a starting point, not a rule.

| Your situation | Cofounder | Agency | Freelancer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idea only, testing whether anyone cares | Not yet | Possible for a small, paid prototype | Often the best fit for a narrow prototype |
| Software is the product and you plan to raise venture capital | Usually yes, before or soon after the raise | To build version one while you recruit | For specific pieces |
| Software supports a business that earns money another way (a clinic, a trade, a retailer) | Rarely | Usually the best fit | For small, defined jobs |
| You have revenue or a pilot customer and need a production version quickly | Helpful, not required | Usually the best fit | If the scope is small and you can manage it |
| Version one exists and needs continuous development for years | Yes, or a senior first hire | For surges and specialist work | For specific pieces |

The common thread: a cofounder is about **who will keep building the company**, while an agency or freelancer is about **getting a defined piece of software built**. Those are different problems.

## What does a technical cofounder give you that an agency can't?

**Commitment, shared risk and technical judgement at the founding table.** A cofounder thinks about the product when nobody is paying them to, makes trade offs with you as an owner, and stays when the first version turns out to be wrong.

Specifically, a good technical cofounder brings:

- **Speed of iteration after launch.** Small changes ship the same day without a change request or a new quote.
- **Credibility with investors.** Many early stage investors want to see that the team can build without outsourcing its core product.
- **Recruiting.** Engineers often join because of who they'll work with. A respected technical founder makes the first hires easier.
- **Low cash burn.** A cofounder on little or no salary stretches your runway, in exchange for equity.
- **Ownership of the architecture.** Decisions about the technology are made by someone who'll live with them for years.

None of that comes free. The price is equity, shared control and the risk that a founder relationship breaks down, which is harder to end than a supplier contract.

## What does an agency or freelancer give you that a cofounder can't?

**Speed to a defined result, a known cost, and no permanent claim on your company.** You pay for the work, you own the result once paid, and the relationship ends cleanly if it isn't working.

| | Agency | Freelancer |
|---|---|---|
| Who does the work | A team: design, frontend, backend, testing, project management | One person, sometimes with subcontractors |
| Best for | A complete version one with a start and an end | A well defined piece, such as a prototype or a feature |
| Cost model | Fixed price or time and materials | Usually hourly or daily |
| Who manages it | The agency's project lead, with your decisions | You |
| Continuity risk | Lower: the team outlasts any one person | Higher: if they're unavailable, work stops |
| Equity | None, if you pay cash | None, if you pay cash |

For the wider trade offs between these two and hiring your own developers, see [freelancer vs agency vs in-house](/guides/freelancer-vs-agency-vs-in-house).

What an agency won't do is care about your company the way a founder does. It won't pitch investors, work weekends for equity, or decide on its own to rebuild a feature because a customer complained. If your plan depends on someone doing those things, you need a cofounder or an early employee, not a supplier.

## Equity or cash: what does each really cost?

**Equity looks cheap at the start and becomes the most expensive way to pay if the company succeeds.** Cash costs you now; equity costs you a share of every future outcome.

A simple illustration. These are assumed numbers to show the shape of the trade off, not advice or a valuation.

| | Pay cash for an MVP | Give a cofounder 20% |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | The build, for example $90,000 | Little or no cash |
| If the company fails | You've lost the cash | Nobody gets anything |
| If the company is later worth $5 million | You still own the 20% | The cofounder's stake is worth $1 million |
| If the company is later worth $50 million | You still own the 20% | The cofounder's stake is worth $10 million |
| After version one | You still need someone to keep building | They keep building |

The last row is the real comparison. A cofounder isn't paid only for version one; they're paid for years of building after it. If you pay cash for the MVP, you still need a plan for who builds next, and that person will cost salary, equity or both.

For context on what that person costs in the market: Robert Walters' 2026 salary guide lists NSW permanent salaries of $150,000 to $170,000 for a senior full stack developer, $175,000 to $190,000 for a lead or principal full stack developer, and $225,000 to $350,000 for a CTO, excluding superannuation. An experienced engineer who joins as a cofounder on little or no salary is giving up that income, which is part of why they expect meaningful equity.

## How do founders structure a cofounder deal safely?

**Use vesting, a shareholders agreement and an IP assignment, and get them drafted by a lawyer before serious work starts.** The paperwork is cheap compared with a dispute over who owns the code.

The usual building blocks:

1. **A company, not an informal arrangement.** Equity, investment and grants generally need an incorporated company.
2. **A shareholders agreement** covering roles, decision making, what happens if someone leaves, and how new shares are issued.
3. **Vesting** so shares are earned over time, often with a cliff before the first shares vest. Unvested shares return if a founder leaves early.
4. **An IP assignment** so everything the cofounder creates for the business, including code written before incorporation, belongs to the company. Investors check this in due diligence.
5. **Clear time commitment.** Full time, part time, or until a funding milestone, written down.

If you later offer shares or options to employees, the ATO's employee share scheme rules apply. Under the start-up concession, an eligible employee can reduce the taxable discount on their ESS interests to nil, but the company must meet conditions such as being unlisted, incorporated for less than 10 years and below a turnover threshold, and the interests generally have to be held for at least three years. Those rules are for employees, not a substitute for advice on founder shares. Speak to a startup lawyer and an accountant before you issue anything.

## What hybrid paths work?

**The most common path is to pay for a focused first version, use it to get evidence, then bring in a cofounder or senior hire on better terms.** Evidence changes negotiations: a candidate looking at paying users takes less risk than one looking at a pitch deck.

| Path | How it works | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Agency MVP, then first senior hire | Agency builds version one; you hire a senior engineer to take it over | Choose a stack you can hire for, and require documentation and a handover |
| Freelancer prototype, then cofounder | A freelancer builds a narrow prototype to test demand; you recruit a cofounder with that evidence | Treat the prototype as disposable if the cofounder wants to rebuild |
| Cofounder plus agency | A technical cofounder leads; an agency adds capacity for a set scope | Make the cofounder the decision maker, not the agency |
| Fractional CTO plus agency | A part time senior adviser oversees an agency build | Make sure the adviser can actually judge the code, not just the plan |

Whichever path you take, insist that the code is in a repository the company controls and that the IP is assigned to the company. ## What should you ask a potential cofounder or an agency?

**Ask questions that reveal how they'll behave when things go wrong, not just what they've built.**

For a potential technical cofounder:

- Can we work on a small project together for a few weeks before we commit?
- How much time can you give, and what happens to that if you get a job offer?
- What would you build first, and what would you deliberately not build?
- What equity and vesting do you have in mind, and why?
- Have you led a product from nothing to paying users before?

For an agency or freelancer:

- Who exactly will work on this, and how senior are they?
- Is the price fixed, and what is excluded?
- When and how does the code and IP become ours?
- How will a future in-house developer take this over?
- Do you take equity? (If they do, treat them like a cofounder candidate, with the same paperwork.)
