## Is my app quote reasonable? A quick test

**A quote is reasonable if it falls inside the market range for its type of build and the vendor can show you the hours behind it.** Start with the band. If your quote sits well outside it, find out which assumptions differ before you decide anyone is wrong.

| Type of build | Published Australian range (AUD, ex GST) | What a quote in this band should normally cover |
|---|---|---|
| Simple app or MVP | $20,000 to $50,000 | One core workflow, standard design components, a basic backend |
| Standard business app | $50,000 to $120,000 | iOS and Android, login and roles, admin tools, one or two integrations |
| Feature rich platform | $120,000 to $250,000 | Several user types, payments, several integrations, reporting |
| Simple internal software tool | $15,000 to $40,000 | One team, one workflow, low sensitivity data |
| Mid complexity business system | $40,000 to $150,000 | Customer portal, job management, quoting and invoicing |

The app bands come from AppGurus' 2026 guide and the software bands from Expeed's 2026 guide. Wave Digital puts most production ready MVPs at $125,000 to $250,000 or more, which shows how much the definition of "MVP" moves the number. These are market ranges, not quotes, and our [app development cost guide](/guides/app-development-cost-australia) and [custom software cost guide](/guides/custom-software-development-cost-australia) explain what pushes a project from one band to the next.

Then apply three checks:

1. **Is the scope written down?** If the quote is a single number with a paragraph of description, it isn't comparable with anything.
2. **Can you see the hours?** Effort by feature or by phase lets you see where the money goes.
3. **Is GST stated?** Business quotes are usually ex GST. Add 10% to compare like with like, and ask your accountant whether you can claim it back.

## Why do quotes for the same app differ so much?

**Because each vendor is pricing a different app in their head.** Rates in Australia sit in a fairly narrow band; the number of hours each vendor assumes does not. Conduct reports small and medium Australian agencies at about $123 to $330 an hour, while AppGurus puts typical local rates at $120 to $200. A tenfold gap in totals almost always means a large gap in assumed scope.

The usual sources of the gap:

- **Backend and admin.** A cheap quote may cover the screens a customer sees and nothing behind them. Someone still has to build the database, the API and the tools staff use to manage users, content and orders.
- **Design depth.** Template components cost far less than a custom design system with user testing.
- **Integrations.** "Connects to Xero" can mean pushing one invoice or a full sync with error handling.
- **Testing.** Conduct's budget split puts quality assurance at 15 to 20% of a project. A quote with no testing line has moved that cost to you.
- **Who does the work.** Onshore, offshore or a mix, and how senior the people are. Our [onshore vs offshore guide](/guides/onshore-vs-offshore-software-development) covers that trade off.
- **Risk.** A vendor quoting a fixed price on a vague brief adds contingency. A vendor quoting low on a vague brief may plan to recover it through variations.
- **After launch.** Warranty, hosting setup, app store submission and handover are included by some vendors and excluded by others.

## What should a complete software quote include?

**A complete quote lets you answer three questions without asking: what you get, what it costs in total, and what happens when something changes.** Use this checklist on every quote you receive.

| Item | What good looks like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Written scope | User types, main journeys, screens or features, integrations | Without it, the price has no anchor |
| Exclusions | A written list of what is not included | This is where most surprise costs hide |
| Assumptions | Who supplies content, data, API access, test devices | A broken assumption becomes a variation |
| Effort breakdown | Hours, days or cost by feature or phase | Lets you compare vendors and cut scope sensibly |
| Testing | Automated tests, manual testing, who tests on which devices | Untested code costs more after launch |
| Deployment | Environments, hosting setup, app store submission | Often missing from cheap quotes |
| Timeline and milestones | Dates or durations for each stage you can review | Gives you checkpoints before the next payment |
| Payment schedule | Payments tied to milestones | Keeps payment and progress in step |
| Change process | How new requests are priced and approved, in writing | Stops scope creep becoming bill creep |
| Warranty | A defined period of defect fixes after launch | Shows what is fixed without a new invoice |
| Ownership | When the IP is assigned and when the code and accounts are handed over | Decides whether you can move to another team later |
| Ongoing costs | Hosting, licences, store fees, support options | The build price is not the total cost |

If a vendor can't fill in a row, that isn't always a deal breaker. It is a question to ask before you sign. Our [contract checklist](/guides/software-development-contract-checklist) goes deeper on the ownership, warranty and liability rows.

## What are the red flags in a software quote?

**The biggest red flags are missing scope, payment that runs ahead of progress, and silence on ownership.** Any one of these deserves a direct question. Three or more together usually means the quote isn't comparable with the others.

1. **No scope document.** A price without a written scope is a guess you have agreed to pay.
2. **The whole price upfront.** Paying everything before work starts leaves you with nothing to hold back if delivery slips.
3. **No IP or ownership clause.** Under Australian copyright law the author owns the code by default, so silence favours the vendor. See [who owns the source code](/guides/who-owns-the-source-code-australia).
4. **No testing line.** Either testing is hidden in other lines or it isn't happening.
5. **Vague items.** "Integration", "backend" or "admin panel" as a single line with a single price, and no detail.
6. **Hourly billing with no estimate or cap.** That is time and materials with none of the controls that should go with it.
7. **No warranty.** If defects found after launch are billed as new work, the vendor has no reason to test well.
8. **A price that drops sharply when you push back, with no change in scope.** It suggests the first number wasn't built from the work.
9. **The vendor keeps the accounts.** Domains, hosting, app store listings and databases should be registered to you.
10. **One sided terms.** Since 9 November 2023, the ACCC says unfair terms in standard form contracts are banned for small businesses with fewer than 100 employees or under $10 million in annual turnover. Its examples include terms that let only one party end the contract or limit its responsibilities.

None of this proves a vendor is acting in bad faith. Some good small teams simply write thin quotes. The fix is the same either way: ask for the missing pieces in writing.

## Which questions expose missing scope?

**Ask questions that force the vendor to describe the work, not the price.** Their answers tell you what they assumed and how well they understood your business.

- Walk me through what a customer does from opening the app to finishing the main task. What did you assume at each step?
- Who manages users, content and orders, and through what screens?
- What exactly does the integration with our existing system do, in which direction, and what happens when it fails?
- How many hours have you allowed for testing, and on which devices?
- What will we need to pay for each month after launch?
- What is excluded from this price?
- What happens if we ask for a change halfway through?
- Who will write the code, and can we meet them?
- When does the code become ours, and when do we receive the repository and every login?

A vendor who answers quickly and specifically has usually built something similar before. A vendor who says "we'll work that out later" is telling you the price will change later too.

## How do you compare quotes like for like?

**Write one scope, send it to every vendor, then score the responses with weights you set before you open them.** Setting the weights first stops the lowest total from deciding the outcome by default.

Copy this matrix. Score each quote from 1 to 5 on each row, multiply by the weight, and add up the totals.

| Criterion | Weight | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Scope coverage | 25% | Every feature in your scope is priced, with exclusions listed |
| Total cost over two years | 20% | Build plus hosting, licences and support, all stated |
| Delivery risk | 15% | Milestones you can test, payments tied to them, a realistic timeline |
| Ownership and exit | 15% | IP assigned as paid for, clear handover of code and accounts |
| Team and evidence | 15% | You've met the people doing the work and seen comparable work |
| Quality practices | 10% | Testing, code review, security checks and a warranty described |

**A worked example (hypothetical vendors and figures).** A business sends one scope for a booking app to three vendors.

| | Vendor A | Vendor B | Vendor C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quoted build price (ex GST) | $38,000 | $96,000 | $165,000 |
| What the quote covers | Customer screens only; admin and integration excluded | Customer app, admin portal, payment and calendar integration, testing, 30 day warranty | Same as B, plus a custom design system, discovery workshops and six months of support |
| Weighted score out of 5 | 2.3 | 4.2 | 3.9 |

Vendor A looks cheapest, but adding the excluded admin portal and integration through a second vendor would likely push the total past Vendor B. Vendor C is the most complete, and if the business needs the six months of support anyway, its real gap to B is smaller than the headline. Vendor B wins on these weights; a business with a bigger brand budget might reasonably weight design higher and pick C.

The point of the matrix isn't precision. It makes the trade offs visible, so you choose deliberately.

## When is the cheaper quote the right one?

**The cheaper quote is the right one when it covers your actual scope and the vendor can show how they'll deliver it.** Low price isn't a red flag on its own.

It is often the right call when:

- The job is small and clearly defined, such as one internal tool for one team.
- You're testing an idea and plan to rebuild once it proves demand. Our [MVP cost guide](/guides/mvp-development-cost-australia) covers how to cut scope sensibly.
- The vendor has built the same kind of thing before and can reuse proven parts.
- A freelancer or small team suits the work and you have someone on your side to manage it. The [freelancer vs agency guide](/guides/freelancer-vs-agency-vs-in-house) sets out when that works.
- An existing SaaS product or low code tool covers most of the need. Sometimes the cheapest quote is a subscription, not a build.

It is usually the wrong call when the low price comes from excluding work you need, from fixed pricing on a vague brief, or from terms that leave the vendor owning your code.
