## When should a Sydney business build custom software?

**Build custom software when the way you work is a real advantage or when off-the-shelf tools force you into expensive workarounds.** If your process is standard (payroll, basic CRM, accounting), buy a SaaS product and configure it. Custom work earns its cost in the gaps: the operational system that ties three SaaS tools together, the client portal that reflects how your firm actually works, or the internal platform that replaces a tangle of spreadsheets.

A quick decision matrix:

| Situation | Usually the better choice |
|---|---|
| Standard process, many vendors sell it, you'd accept their way of working | Buy SaaS |
| SaaS covers most of it but integration is the pain | Buy SaaS, build the integration layer |
| Staff re-key data between systems daily | Custom integration or workflow tool |
| The software is part of the product your customers pay for | Build custom |
| Strict data location, audit or access rules that SaaS can't meet | Build custom, hosted in your own cloud account |
| Budget under about $30k and a tight deadline | Low-code or SaaS first, revisit later |

Our [build vs buy guide](/guides/build-vs-buy-software) works through total cost of ownership in more detail, including when buying is clearly the better answer.

## What does custom software cost in Sydney?

**Sydney rates for senior software talent are the highest of the major Australian cities, and they set the floor for what Sydney agencies charge.** Re:Sourced's 2026 guide puts senior software contractor day rates in Sydney at $800 to $1,100 (AUD, ex GST), with principal and specialist profiles reaching about $1,300, compared with $750 to $1,050 in Melbourne and $700 to $950 in Brisbane.

Here's the arithmetic that turns rates into budgets. These are illustrative ranges, not quotes.

| Example scope | Senior effort (person-days) | At $800 to $1,100 per day |
|---|---|---|
| Integration between two existing systems with an admin screen | 30 to 50 | $24k to $55k |
| Internal operations tool replacing spreadsheets, with roles and reporting | 80 to 150 | $64k to $165k |
| Customer-facing portal with payments and integrations | 150 to 300 | $120k to $330k |

Agencies add design, project management and testing on top, and some price below contractor rates by using junior or offshore staff. Ongoing maintenance typically runs well after launch, so budget for it from the start. ## How do NSW Government agencies buy custom software?

**NSW agencies buy ICT through buy.nsw, and the ICT Services Scheme is mandatory for ICT goods and services under Procurement Board Direction PBD-2021-04, unless another mandated whole-of-government arrangement applies.** For a supplier, that means being on the scheme is usually a precondition to being engaged directly.

Key facts from buy.nsw at the time of writing:

- The scheme covers 18 categories, from architecture and design through software and cloud services.
- Suppliers apply at any time through buy.nsw, and applications are typically processed in one to two weeks.
- **Registered** suppliers can take low-risk contracts up to $150,000 ex GST. **Advanced** suppliers, who provide referee reports and pass solvency checks, can take high-risk work or contracts above $150,000.
- ICT consulting engagements up to $250,000 ex GST can be negotiated directly with a supplier that accepts the ICT consulting commercial framework, without three quotes.
- The ICT Purchasing Framework uses Core& contracts for low-risk procurements up to $1 million and the MICTA/ICTA framework for high-risk procurements above $1 million.

If you're an agency buyer, check scheme status before anything else. If you're a Sydney software vendor wanting to sell to government, our guide to [selling software to Australian government](/guides/selling-software-to-australian-government) covers the state and federal pathways.

## Which NSW privacy laws affect the software you build?

**NSW agencies are governed by the Privacy and Personal Information Protection Act 1998 (PPIP Act); health information in NSW is governed by the Health Records and Information Privacy Act 2002 (HRIP Act), which reaches the private sector too.** Private businesses outside health are generally covered by the Commonwealth Privacy Act 1988 instead.

| Law | Who it binds | What it means for the software |
|---|---|---|
| PPIP Act 1998 (NSW) | NSW agencies, statutory authorities, councils, universities, state-owned corporations | Build to the Information Protection Principles; support the Mandatory Notification of Data Breach scheme |
| HRIP Act 2002 (NSW) | Public agencies and private organisations that provide health services or hold health information, including some businesses with turnover over $3 million | Build to the Health Privacy Principles; access, correction and retention features matter |
| Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) | Most private businesses with turnover over $3 million, all private health service providers | Build to the APPs; new automated decision disclosure rules from 10 December 2026 |

Since 28 November 2023, NSW agencies bound by the PPIP Act must notify the Privacy Commissioner and affected individuals of eligible data breaches. For software, that turns into concrete requirements: know what personal information each system holds, log access so you can scope a breach quickly, and be able to identify who was affected. We treat those as design inputs, not afterthoughts. The OAIC also notes that in NSW, private health providers must comply with both federal and state privacy law when handling health information.

## What should a Sydney software contract protect?

**Whoever you hire, the contract should leave you able to walk away with a working system.** Sydney has a crowded market of agencies and freelancers, and the gap between a good contract and a weak one matters more than the gap between two good developers. Check for these before signing:

1. **IP assignment.** New code and designs created for you are assigned to you, with pre-existing tools listed separately and licensed to you.
2. **Source code access from day one.** The repository sits in your account, not the developer's, so you never have to ask for it.
3. **Credentials and accounts in your name.** Cloud hosting, domains and third-party services are owned by you, with the developer as a user.
4. **Clear acceptance criteria.** What "done" means for each milestone, and how defects found after acceptance are handled.
5. **Warranty period.** A defined window in which the developer fixes defects at no charge.
6. **Exit and handover.** Documentation, a handover session and removal of the developer's access at the end.
7. **Privacy and security terms.** How personal information is handled, where it's hosted, and breach notification obligations that match your own under the Privacy Act or PPIP Act.
