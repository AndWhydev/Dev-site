Does the Privacy Act apply to small business? A guide for app and software owners

Last updated 8 November 2026 Published by AWLabs How we write

The short answer

Most Australian businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less are exempt from the Privacy Act 1988, but many aren't. The OAIC lists businesses covered regardless of turnover, including health service providers, businesses that trade in personal information, Commonwealth contractors, AML/CTF reporting entities (which since 1 July 2026 include many lawyers, accountants and real estate businesses), CDR accredited businesses and businesses related to a covered one. The statutory privacy tort, in force since 10 June 2025, can reach businesses that aren't covered at all. The 2026 reform exposure draft keeps the exemption, but changes how trading in personal information works. Build your app as if you're covered: it usually costs little and customers increasingly expect it.

Key takeaways

  • The exemption turns on annual turnover of $3 million or less, counted from all income sources, in every financial year since 2002, and it's lost entirely if any exception applies.
  • Health service providers are covered whatever their size. That catches many clinics, allied health practices and wellness apps that hold health information.
  • AML/CTF reporting entities are covered regardless of turnover. From 1 July 2026 that list grew to include many lawyers, accountants, conveyancers and real estate businesses.
  • The statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy applies to individuals and entities that aren't APP entities, so exempt businesses can still be sued for serious privacy invasions.
  • The Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026 exposure draft doesn't remove the small business exemption. It remains subject to further consideration by government.
  • Even if you're exempt, a privacy policy, minimal collection, good security and a breach plan are cheap to build in and often required by bigger customers and app stores.

Does the Privacy Act apply to small businesses?

Usually not, if your annual turnover is $3 million or less, but the exceptions are wide enough that many small businesses with an app are covered anyway. The OAIC’s own summary is that most small businesses are not covered by the Privacy Act 1988, but some are.

The test has two parts. First, turnover: a small business is one with annual turnover of $3 million or less, and the OAIC’s checklist asks whether you’ve exceeded $3 million in any financial year since 2002. Turnover means all income from all sources, but not assets held, capital gains or proceeds of capital sales. Second, the exceptions: if any one applies, you’re covered regardless of turnover.

If you’re covered, you have to comply with the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme applies to you. If you’re not, you still have reasons to handle personal information carefully, covered below.

Which small businesses are covered regardless of turnover?

The OAIC lists the categories that bring a small business under the Privacy Act whatever its size. Several of them are common among businesses that build apps.

The OAIC also notes that some small businesses have other obligations under the Privacy Act even outside the APPs, such as the credit reporting rules in Part IIIA and the Privacy (Tax File Number) Rule 2015 for handling tax file numbers.

The 2026 AML/CTF change

This is the change small professional firms most often miss. AUSTRAC says that from 1 July 2026, businesses including lawyers, accountants, conveyancers, real estate professionals and dealers in precious stones and metals come under the AML/CTF regime, taking the number of regulated businesses from around 19,000 to close to 100,000. The OAIC lists AML/CTF reporting entities among the businesses the Privacy Act covers regardless of turnover.

So a small accounting practice or real estate agency that became a reporting entity this year should check with its lawyer how far the Privacy Act now applies to it. If you build or run software for these firms, such as client onboarding, identity checks or document portals, design it to the APPs.

Health service providers

Health is the other common trap. A small practice, a telehealth startup or a wellness app can be a health service provider, and health information is also “sensitive information” under the Privacy Act, with stricter rules on collection. Whether your app is a health service depends on what it does, so get advice before launch rather than after.

What do tranche 1 and tranche 2 change?

Tranche 1 is law and partly in force; tranche 2 is a draft that keeps the small business exemption. The timeline as at 8 November 2026:

What the tranche 2 exposure draft does

The Attorney-General’s Department says the bill “remains subject to further consideration by government”. Gadens’ summary notes that the draft introduces a single fair and reasonable test for collecting, using and disclosing personal information, that the small business exemption for businesses with turnover under $3 million has not been removed, and that commencement dates and transition periods are still blank. Gadens also reports that the Government intends to introduce the legislation before the end of 2026.

For small businesses, the most relevant drafted change is to the trading exception. The exposure draft rewrites subsection 6D(8) so that a business collecting personal information disclosed to it as a trade can remain a small business operator if it collects the information with the individual’s consent or as required or authorised by law. If your business model involves buying or selling personal information, such as lead generation, watch how this is finalised.

Can an exempt business still get into trouble?

Yes. Being outside the APPs doesn’t put you outside every privacy risk. Three examples:

  1. The statutory tort. Since 10 June 2025, individuals can sue for serious invasions of privacy, and the OAIC says the tort reaches individuals and entities that may not be APP entities. Remedies can include damages, an injunction or an order to apologise.
  2. Contracts. A larger customer, a government agency or an enterprise partner will usually require you to handle their data to the APPs or better, notify breaches within set times, and keep data in particular locations. Breaching those terms is a contract problem whatever the Privacy Act says.
  3. Other laws. Spam, telemarketing, surveillance, health records and consumer law apply independently of the small business exemption, and some states have their own health records laws.

What should your app or software do anyway?

Build to the core privacy practices from the start, because they cost little at design time and a lot to retrofit. This checklist assumes you may be covered now, or later, or by contract:

What if your app uses AI?

Using AI doesn’t change whether you’re exempt, but it changes how much personal information flows and where it goes. Sending customer conversations, documents or records to an AI model is a disclosure to that provider, often overseas. If you’re covered, APP 8 applies; if you’re not, your customers and partners will still ask.

Practical steps: check where the model provider processes and stores data, whether it uses your data for training, and whether an Australian region is available; strip or mask personal information before it reaches the model where you can; and note any AI processing in your privacy policy. Our guide to the Privacy Act and AI goes into detail, and AI for small business covers the wider decisions.

What if a bigger customer makes you sign privacy terms?

Read them as the real standard you’ll be held to, because for many small software businesses the contract is stricter than the law. Common clauses:

  • Comply with the APPs as if you were an APP entity
  • Notify the customer of any suspected breach within a short window, sometimes 24 to 72 hours
  • Keep data in Australia, or in named locations
  • Use only approved subcontractors, including cloud and AI providers
  • Allow audits or answer security questionnaires
  • Delete or return data when the contract ends

Before signing, list the services your software uses and where they process data, and check your logs could tell you what was accessed in an incident. If you can’t meet a clause, negotiate it before you sign rather than discovering it during a breach.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Privacy Act apply to my small business?

If your annual turnover has been $3 million or less in every financial year since 2002, the Privacy Act usually doesn't apply, unless one of the exceptions does. The OAIC lists them, including health service providers, businesses that trade in personal information, Commonwealth contractors, AML/CTF reporting entities and businesses that have opted in. Use the OAIC's small business checklist, or ask your lawyer.

How is the $3 million turnover calculated?

The OAIC says annual turnover for the Privacy Act includes all income from all sources, but not assets held, capital gains or the proceeds of capital sales. If your business hasn't operated for a whole financial year, you project a full year figure from the income so far.

My app collects health information but I'm a tiny startup. Am I covered?

Possibly. The Privacy Act covers health service providers regardless of turnover, and whether you are one depends on what your service does, not your size. Apps that record, assess or help manage someone's health are worth checking carefully with a lawyer before launch.

Do I still need a privacy policy if I'm exempt?

The Privacy Act may not require one, but you'll usually need one anyway. Apple's App Store Review Guidelines say all apps must include a link to their privacy policy, many enterprise and government customers require privacy terms in their contracts, and payment, analytics and marketing tools often require a policy as a condition of use.

What is tranche 2 of the privacy reforms?

It's the second stage of reform following the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. The Attorney-General's Department released an exposure draft, the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026, for consultation until 18 September 2026. It proposes a new fair and reasonable test for handling personal information, but keeps the small business exemption. It isn't law yet.

Can an exempt small business be sued for a privacy breach?

It can face a claim under the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, which commenced on 10 June 2025. The OAIC says the tort is broader in application than the Privacy Act and extends to individuals and entities that may not be APP entities. The tort has its own thresholds, so get legal advice on any specific situation.

Can a small business opt in to the Privacy Act?

Yes. The OAIC lists businesses that have opted in among those covered regardless of turnover. Some small businesses do this to reassure customers or meet a contract requirement. Once you opt in, you have to comply with the Australian Privacy Principles.
